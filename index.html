02 Context & role

The property and my role

The property is a 23-room hotel in the historic centre of Granada, run by a team of seven. I joined as Assistant Manager in August 2024.

The role covers day-to-day operational management, and full operational responsibility when the Manager is off-site. In practice, that includes:

Front Office Reservations Guest incidents Housekeeping supervision Staff scheduling Onboarding & training SOP development Supplier management Cash handling Process improvement

I also take part in pricing and revenue decisions alongside the Manager, who retains final responsibility there, and I've been involved in evaluating the property's PMS and channel-management tools.

Since joining, I've written and introduced a number of SOPs and operational checklists covering guest-facing and housekeeping processes, and trained the team to use them. This page documents that work: what changed in operations, what guests wrote afterwards, and how I looked at that feedback at a scale that wouldn't be practical by hand.

About this page

This is a personal professional project, not an official property report. It looks at what changed and what guests said, and it does not claim to prove cause and effect. Tools such as ChatGPT and Claude helped me process and organise information; the operational context, the questions and the interpretation are mine. How I did the analysis, and where it falls short, is in the last section.

A necessary caveat

Almost every rated category improved after August 2024, not only staff service. Value-for-money rose the most (+6.1%), staff service rose the least (+2.7%) of the six categories. My role as assistant manager already covered protocols across cleaning, guest service and reservations, not just the guest-facing conversations reviews mention (see Context & role), so a broad-based rise like this is consistent with that wider scope. Even so, this before/after comparison is observational, not experimental: occupancy, pricing, season, guest mix and staffing all changed across the same window and aren't controlled for. The difference in average score is statistically distinguishable from zero under both a two-sample t-test and a Mann-Whitney U test (a non-parametric check appropriate for a discrete 1 to 10 rating scale), but that speaks to the reliability of the measurement, not to what caused it. Review data alone can't establish that any one person, or any one change, caused the shift. That's why the next sections look at more specific evidence too: reviews that name someone directly, and a clear separation of what the data shows from what it can't establish.

05 Guest feedback

What guests specifically
say about my work

44 of 2,611 reviews name me directly by first name. What follows is aggregated and paraphrased. No guest quotations are published, and the underlying reviews are kept private (see Privacy and guest data).

Why 44, not more

Every one of the 44 mentions was checked by hand to rule out guests who share my first name writing about themselves; none did. All 44 fall after August 2024; none appear before, which lines up with when I started. A further 14 reviews describe a young male receptionist without naming anyone. I've kept those separate from the figures above rather than folding them in: at least one predates my start date, which shows that description alone isn't unique to me, so I can't treat the group as confirmed.

06 The evidence

What the evidence shows, and what it doesn't

Across 44 direct mentions, the same few things come up again and again, in different languages, from guests who never spoke to each other. Each point below is labelled as something the data shows, something consistent with the evidence, or something that cannot be established.

Factors this analysis doesn't control for

Seasonality, occupancy, pricing changes, shifts in guest mix, staffing beyond my own role, property changes unrelated to my work, review-selection effects (only guests who complete a stay and choose to review are represented), and the difference in sample size and duration between the before and after periods. None of these are measured here, so none of them can be ruled in or out. I'd rather say so plainly: a case study that names its blind spots is more useful than one that doesn't have any.

08 How I did it

How I looked at
the feedback

The question came first: did guest feedback change after I took on this role, and what did guests actually say about the service? I used 2,611 Booking.com reviews from September 2023 to September 2026 to find out. The tools came second.

From reviews to evidence

What each review contains

Every review carries an overall score (1 to 10) plus six category scores Booking.com collects directly from guests. These category scores aren't read out of the text; they're what guests explicitly rated.

Language diversity

Guests wrote in more than 20 different languages. The recurring themes identified in direct mentions earlier on this page appear across several of these languages rather than being concentrated in a single linguistic group.

Limitations

Using the feedback day to day

This page looks backward, but the same approach works going forward. As new procedures went in (a new cleaning checklist, a different check-in script, updated advice for guests at reception), rereading the reviews period by period became a practical way to check whether a change was actually landing, not just sounding good in a meeting. A category score moving, a complaint theme fading out, a new one appearing: those were signals to keep a change, adjust it, or drop it, without waiting for a formal review. Doing that by hand across hundreds of multilingual reviews wasn't realistic, so I used AI tools to help me do it regularly.
